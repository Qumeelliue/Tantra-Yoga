# Definition

The **Five Fundamental Principles of PROUT** are stated in Ánanda Sútram
**5-12 to 5-16**. (Per the editors' note, these five aphorisms correspond to the
five principles Sarkar first gave in English in 1959; the bracketed English under
each Sanskrit sūtra is the author's own wording, not an editorial translation.)

# Explanation

1. **No accumulation without collective approval (5-12)** —
   *"Samājādeshena vinā dhanasaiñcayah akartavyah."* No individual may accumulate
   physical wealth without the clear permission of the **collective body**. The
   universe is the collective property of all; people have *usufructuary* rights
   but none may misuse it — excessive accumulation curtails others' happiness and
   is "flagrantly antisocial."
2. **Maximum utilization & rational distribution of all potentialities (5-13)** —
   *"Sthūlasūkṣmakāraṇeṣu caramopayogah… vaṇṭanaiñca."* The wealth of the **crude,
   subtle and causal** worlds — and all resources of the quinquelemental world
   (solid, liquid, luminous, aerial, ethereal — the
   [five fundamental factors](/cosmology/five-fundamental-factors.md)) — should be
   fully developed and **rationally distributed**.
3. **Maximum utilization of unit & collective potentialities (5-14)** —
   *"Vyaṣṭisamaṣṭi… caramo'payogashca."* The **physical, metaphysical and
   spiritual** potentialities of individual *and* collective bodies must be
   maximally developed. "Collective welfare lies in individuals and individual
   welfare lies in collectivity."
4. **Proper adjustment among utilizations (5-15)** —
   *"Sthūlasūkṣma kāraṇo'payogāh susantulitāh vidheyāh."* A **balanced** policy
   among physical/mental/spiritual and crude/subtle/causal utilizations; society
   takes service per a person's most-developed capacity, and **social control
   belongs to those who are spiritually elevated, intelligent and brave at once**
   (the [sadvipra](/prout/sadvipra.md)).
5. **Progressive utilization, varying with time/space/person (5-16)** —
   *"Deshakālapātraeh upayogāh parivarttante te upayogāh pragatishiilāh
   bhaveyuh."* The **method of utilization changes with [time, space and
   person](/cosmology/relativity.md)** and must be **progressive** — using
   developed science and progressive ideas to extract ever more service from the
   same potential, rather than clinging to "the skeletons of the past."

These close with PROUT's proclamation (see [PROUT](/prout/prout.md)): the theory is
"propounded for the happiness and all-round welfare of all."

# The 1959 English original — *The Cosmic Brotherhood* (II-11)

The **earliest** statement of the five principles is the prose of the 1959 chapter
*The Cosmic Brotherhood* (Idea and Ideology, ch. 11; per the editors' note the five
principles were added to the first edition of *Idea and Ideology* as it was being
printed in **November 1959**). Sarkar there states: "The principles of Prout depend
upon the following fundamental factors:"

1. **No accumulation without collective approval** — "No individual should be
   allowed to accumulate any physical wealth without the clear permission or
   approval of the collective body."
2. **Maximum utilization & rational distribution of all potentialities** — "There
   should be maximum utilization and rational distribution of all mundane,
   supramundane and spiritual potentialities of the universe."
3. **Maximum utilization of unit & collective potentialities** — "There should be
   maximum utilization of physical, metaphysical and spiritual potentialities of
   unit and collective bodies of human society."
4. **Proper adjustment among utilizations** — "There should be a proper adjustment
   amongst these physical, metaphysical, mundane, supramundane and spiritual
   utilizations."
5. **Progressive utilization, varying with time/space/person** — "The method of
   utilization should vary in accordance with changes in time, space and person, and
   the utilization should be of progressive nature."

"Hence, ours is a Progressive Utilization Theory (Prout)." These 1959 English
phrasings are the **author's own wording** and correspond to the bracketed English
of sūtras 5-12…5-16.

# The fifth principle's speciality (PiaN 13/11)

In *The Speciality of the Fifth Fundamental Principle of Prout* (PiaN 13/11, 1988)
Sarkar grounds the fifth principle (5-16) as the code's flexible edge:

- **Principles stay, application adjusts.** "The principles of PROUT will not change,
  rather the application of PROUT will adjust with the changing circumstances."
  Everything is subject to the relative factors of time, space and person; "if any
  theory does not adjust with this law, then it is sure to die."
- **Change is the law of order.** Examples of dogma that died for rigidity: the title
  *Cakravarty* (no longer honourable), widow-sacrifice in the Ganges, religious bans
  on interest (which preclude banking), the caste system. "If society continues to
  follow the same patterns, it becomes static and dies."
- **The commune-system critique** (communism's deadlock): equal pay for intelligent and
  ordinary workers destroys incentive, so "the capabilities of a genius will not get
  any scope for expression" — the applied theory has "reached a deadlock."
- **Attraction and Neohumanism:** attraction is also a law of nature; its jurisdiction
  must be widened to include plants, birds, animals and inanimate objects — "This is
  Neohumanism and Neohumanism is essential for the all-round development of the human
  mind" (linking 5-16's flexibility to [Neohumanism](/philosophy/neohumanism.md)).

# Notes — fidelity

- The bracketed English under 5-12…5-16 is **Sarkar's own 1959 wording**, not a
  translation — preserve it as primary. "Collective body" (5-12) renders *samāja*
  ("society"); "should vary" (5-16) renders *parivarttante* ("does vary").
- **II-11 is the earliest dated statement** (Nov 1959, per the editors' footnote):
  the five principles are first given *in English prose* here, later canonised as
  sūtras 5-12…5-16 (1961). Keep the prose as the historical-primary formulation;
  note the sūtra versions add the *saṃskṛta* and the Sanskrit-based terminology
  (*caramopayoga*, *guṇānupātena*, etc.) absent from the prose.

# Definition

**Sāhitya** is "that whose characteristic is to move with (*sahita* = "with") the
trends of life" — "the portrait of real life, an external expression of the internal
workings of the mind, a bold and powerful expression of the suppressed sighs of the
human heart." Because *sāhitya* also reads *sa + hita* ("that which co-exists with
*hiṭa* [welfare]"), "where there is no inner spirit of welfare, we cannot use the term
*sāhitya*." The slogan of true literature: **"Art for service and blessedness"** — "The
statement 'Art for art's sake' is not acceptable." (14 February 1957, DNE 2.)

# Explanation

- **Etymology — two readings of one word.** (1) *sahita* = "with": literature is that
  "whose characteristic is to move with the trends of life"; (2) *sa + hita* =
  "that which co-exists with *hiṭa* [welfare]". The second is decisive: "Where there is
  no inner spirit of welfare, we cannot use the term *sāhitya*."
- **Art for service and blessedness.** "Art for art's sake" is rejected outright;
  rather, "Art for service and blessedness." The creations of "literature-dealers" or
  "pot-boilers" who treat society as buyers of books "cannot be treated as *sāhitya*."
- **The sāhityika as kavi — seer of truth.** "In the language of the Vedas, a sāhityika
  is called a *kavi* [seer]. Only seers of truth can create true literature, for the
  task of a sāhityika is to point towards the future, and the ability to look into the
  future belongs to the seers of truth alone." Real sāhityikas "are not only beacons of
  the present, they are also minstrels of the past and messengers of the future"; they
  are "epoch-makers and so they are the sages and seers of society."
- **Yuga sāhitya (epochal) vs. taṭastha sāhitya (coastal).** Epochal literature "expresses
  in clear terms the demands of a particular era; it moves hand in hand with the
  collective psychology" and must be written "in the people's popular language" — the
  mother tongue. Coastal literature is literature "which without severing its
  relationship with us completely, is marching so far ahead of our time that we are not
  really in step with it"; it "maintains the relation between the two, between the
  temporal and the eternal, by standing on this coastline." Epochal literature lasts a
  shorter time but fulfils the era's needs; coastal literature lasts longer but "it is
  less significant, I feel, in fulfilling the needs of a particular era."
- **The Cosmic backdrop.** "The world is the thought projection of the Cosmic Mind, so
  there is no question of even a momentary pause in the eternal flow." Literature is
  "the psychic expression of human dynamism."
- **Attunement to prāṇa dharma.** Artists must "maintain an intimate relation with the
  psychological and cultural structures of the people, with the *prāṇá dharma*
  [innate characteristics] of their existence" — "if artists remain preoccupied with
  floating like balloons in the sky and forget the ordinary people, all their creations
  will end up in smoke."
- **The evolution of svabhāva.** Literature brings about "different steps, different
  phases, of evolution of *svabhāva* [humanity's true nature] through the unique union of
  *shreya* [blissful spiritual union] and *preya* [sensory pleasure]."
- **Sāhityikas — the teachers of society.** (DNE 1) "Actually sāhityikas are a type of
  teacher – they are the teachers of society"; their responsibility is to educate
  children and society at large, in language, drama, cinema and radio that is
  benevolent and free of party or commercial control.

# In *Varna Vijinana* (1983) — sāhitya and literature are not the same

*Varna Vijinana* 8 (*Language and Dialect – 2*, 7 August 1983, Calcutta) sharpens
the same distinction on etymological grounds:

- **Sáhitya means hitena saha [with welfare].** "There is a night and day
  difference between" *sáhitya* and the English word "literature": literature is
  "that art form which is based on letters," while "For it to be sáhitya it must
  imbibe the thought of welfare. Sáhitya means hitena saha [with welfare]." Hence
  "the scope of literature is vaster than the scope of sáhitya… In simple terms,
  all sáhitya is literature but not all literature is sáhitya." The *apasáhitya*
  compositions fall within literature but not sáhitya.
- **Evaluation by a timeless measuring rod.** Rather than "the touchstone of the era
  or the environment," Sarkar would test compositions "by using a measuring rod
  that will stand the test of time."
- **Oral sāhitya.** Sáhitya does not require writing: the Rgveda and Yajurveda were
  memorized (hence the Vedas are called *Shruti*, "that which is learnt by
  hearing"), the Caryápada is "more than a thousand years old," and Bhojpuri,
  Magahii, Chatrisgarii, Maethilii etc. "have their own oral literature" (Maethilii
  literature is nine hundred years old; Jyotiriiśvara Ṭhākur, composer of the
  *Varnaratnákara*, is its father) — so "this oral literature must also be
  respected as literature, regardless of its tangibility or intangibility."
- **Akṣara — the imperishable.** The Sanskrit synonym for "letter" is *akśara*:
  "Akśara means 'that which is not kśara [perishable]'". The Yajurveda verse is
  cited: "Kśaraḿ pradhánamamrtákśaraḿ harah / Kśarátmaná viishate deva ekah"
  [Prakṛti is ever-waning and Puruṣa is changeless and immortal]. "Whatever sound
  has been produced on this earth, whatever song has been sung, will continue to
  reverberate in the cosmic hub for infinite time."

# Notes — fidelity

- **Direct teaching:** "Art for service and blessedness"; "only seers of truth can
  create true literature"; the *yuga/taṭastha* (epochal/coastal) distinction is his own
  binary, kept exactly with its parenthetical Sanskrit.
- **VV 8 (1983) corroborates the welfare-definition** — "Sáhitya means hitena saha
  [with welfare]"; "all sáhitya is literature but not all literature is sáhitya";
  the oral dimension (Vedas as *Shruti*, Caryápada, regional oral literatures) and
  the akṣara etymology with its Yajurveda verse are direct teaching, kept with
  source diacritics.
- **Etymology kept exact:** his two readings — *sahita* = "with" and *sa + hita* =
  "co-exists with welfare" — are both preserved, as are the diacritics (*sāhitya*,
  *taṭastha*, *svabhāva*).
- **Quotation glitch in the source:** the passage "the people's language as a vehicle
  of epochal literature" is retained as-is; no editorial smoothing.
- **Could not verify in the cited passages:** a full typology of Bengali literary
  history; the chapter is prescriptive (what sāhitya must be), not a survey.

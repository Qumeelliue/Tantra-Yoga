# Definition

**Pāpa** is the general term for both sin (against religion or God) and crime
(against a social code); *pātaka* is sin against religion or God. **Pratyavāya** is
"not to do what should be done" — held by the ancient sages as *more abominable than
pāpa*. **Crime** is "going against a recognized legal code." The chapter also gives
the psychology of the **six ripus** (to be controlled) and the **eight pāshas** (to
be resisted). (1986, PiaN 12/2.)

# Explanation

- **The grades of pātaka** by severity:
  1. **Pātaka** (ordinary sin) — its reactions can be exhausted by atonement
     (returning stolen money with interest and apology; sincere forgiveness for hurt
     sentiments).
  2. **Atipātaka** (serious sin) — "has no atonement"; e.g. permanently injuring
     another's future, killing a father (Ajātashatru), treachery in peace talks
     (Śaśāṅka slaying Rājyavardhana). Only lifelong dedication to making good the
     damage, with the victim's heartfelt forgiveness, can absolve it — "but actually
     this rarely happens."
  3. **Mahāpātaka** (extremely serious sin) — whose negative influence **recurs** on
     humanity: e.g. Rāvaṇa abducting Sītā disguised as a sannyāsin (rather than
     directly), so that the housewife still suspects a sannyāsin today.
- **Pratyavāya** — failing to do what should be done: parents not educating their
  children, not arranging marriages of marriageable daughters, not remembering
  benefactors gratefully, and engaging in the opposite.
- **Crime and law.** "Going against a recognized legal code is called crime." A state
  rests on fundamental laws: concerned with the state = the **constitution**; with the
  administration of justice = **social law**; any act ultravires to either is a crime.
  Constitutions, codes and judiciaries vary between peoples — but the **few cardinal
  human principles** should be expanded so that the constitution, code, administration
  and judiciary adjust with them, paving the way for the unity of human society.
- **Ripu and pāsha.** *Ripu* ("enemy") is inherent in the mind and expresses outward —
  six: lust, anger, avarice, blind attachment, vanity, jealousy. *Pāsha* ("fetter")
  originates in the external world and influences the mind — eight, incl. **ghṛṇā**
  (hatred), the reactive mentality against an unwholesome action. *Ripu = intro-external;
  pāsha = extro-internal.*
- **The method:** "the Ripus have got to be controlled and the Pashas must be
  resisted." Resisting a ripu makes it express through another (the bribe-taker's
  suppressed greed becomes anger) — hence regulate, channelize (the alcoholic's
  addiction into music/painting). Pāshas must be fought: "to successfully combat the
  fear instinct, one has to march with rapid steps towards the source of fear." —
  *Pāśhabaddho bhavejjiivo pāśhamukto bhavecchivah.*

# Notes — fidelity

- **Direct teaching:** pratyavāya is *more abominable* than pāpa; atipātaka "has no
  atonement"; mahāpātaka is distinguished by its recurring effect; ripus controlled,
  pāshas resisted. Preserve all distinctions.
- **Ripu vs pāsha** orientation (intro-external vs extro-internal) is Sarkar's own —
  keep it; do not conflate with the classical lists.
- **Could not verify in the cited passage:** a complete enumeration of the eight pāshas
  (only ghṛṇā is named as an example) and of the six ripus' full set is given, but the
  eight pāsha names are not all listed.

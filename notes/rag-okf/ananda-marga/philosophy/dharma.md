# Definition

**Dharma** is *to long for and run after the Great* (*Bṛhadeṣaṇā-praṇidhāna*).
Ánanda Sútram 2-6: *"Brhadeṣaṇāpraṇidhānaṃ ca dharmah"* — "To long for and run
after the Great is dharma."

# Explanation

Knowingly or unknowingly, every human being runs after **limitlessness**. When
one *knowingly* strives to attain the Great and prays to that end, that bearing is
**dharma**, and the effort involved is **dharma sādhanā** (the practice of dharma).

Because (a) happiness is the cherished goal of all, (b) the desire for happiness
is satiated only by attaining limitlessness, and (c) that attainment *is* dharma
sādhanā — therefore dharma sādhanā is **indispensable** for every human (2-7,
*"Tasmāddharmah sadākāryah"*: "dharma should always be practised"). Creatures
inferior to humans cannot do it, their minds being undeveloped; **a human who
does not do it "ill fits the epithet of human being."**

# In the prose discourses

**Dharma as the "holding entity" (dhṛ + man).** The prose gives the etymology
*dhṛ* + suffix *man* — "the holding/containing/controlling entity"
(*Dhṛyate dharma ityāhuh sa eva Paramah Prabhuh*): for every being dharma is the
Supreme, its order overriding all others. Here *dharma* explicitly does **not**
mean "religion" but *svabhāva-dharma* / *guṇadharma* — the essential property by
which a thing is itself; humans did not invent dharma but **discovered** it.

**Vastu, jaeva and Bhāgavata dharma; svadharma vs paradharma.** Dharma in the
expressed world has three tiers: *vastu dharma* (the lawful behaviour of all things
— its codes are physics and chemistry), *jaeva dharma* (the animal functions of
living beings), and **Bhāgavata Dharma = mānava dharma**, the distinctively human
dharma. The first two operate automatically; only Bhāgavata Dharma must be
*consciously practised* — a human who neglects it is "worse than a beast."
Following one's own (human) *svadharma* over an alien *paradharma* rests on the
Gītā's *Svadharme nidhanaṃ shreyah paradharmo bhayāvahah*; abandoning mānava dharma
is a *mahāpātaka*, because the bad example drags others down too.

**Niiti vs dharma; the ten characteristics.** *Niiti* (ethics/morality) is "that
which leads toward *kṣema*" — the **gate**, whereas dharma sādhanā is entering the
house; niiti has both dos and don'ts and blends relative with absolute, while
dharma is pure conduct (*Ācāraṇāt dharmah*), all dos, with Paramātman as its sole
goal. Dharma has **ten characteristics** (*Dhṛti-kṣamā-damo'steyaṃ shaocam
indriyanigrahah / dhiirvidyāsatyamakrodho dashakaṃ dharmalakṣaṇam*): patience,
forgiveness, control of inner enemies, non-stealing, cleanliness, organ-control,
benevolent intellect, spiritual knowledge, truth, non-anger.

# In *Elementary Philosophy* (1955)

Sarkar's first book grounds dharma in both the **word** and **cosmic purpose**.
Etymologically *dharma* signifies **"property"/nature** — as burning is the property of
fire, *"the dharma of humanity is to realize the Infinite or the Cosmic Entity."* Behind
every quest for happiness hides this longing, so *"This alone is the dharma of every
person"*; restated as *"to attain Brahma,"* it becomes the pivot from which Brahma's
existence is then argued. Humans carry **two aspects**, animal and conscious, and *"a
constant duel"* runs between animality (transient enjoyment) and consciousness (the
Infinite). Those who let consciousness lie dormant become *"animals in human form"*; only
those who follow its dictates *"deserve to be called human beings"* — human status is
**earned**, not given.

**How to live (ch.6):** good deeds are **Vidyāmāyā** — *"those which enlarge the reflection
of Consciousness"* while still following Prakṛti's laws — equated with **vaerāgya** and
**viveka**. Sarkar *corrects* vaerāgya: not becoming a recluse or self-mortification but
the **right use** of a thing (*"This right use of a thing is vaerāgya"*); **viveka** is
*"discrimination between good and evil."* Its opposite, **Avidyāmāyā**, dims Consciousness
and breeds the **six enemies** (ṣaḍripu: kāma, krodha, lobha, moha, mada, mātsarya) and
**eight fetters** (aṣṭapāsha). Living rightly therefore means **abstaining from evil
action** — *"the duty of every human being"* — and the swiftest return is by **bhakti**
(bhaj + ktin, devotion). Cosmically, dharma is the order that keeps creation flawless:
*"all things, small or big, follow their own nature (dharma)."*

# In *Human Society* (1959) — morality vs religion

*Moralism* (Human Society 1/1) grounds dharma's distinction from so-called religion in
society's foundation:

- **Dharma (correctly) means the endeavour to attain bliss through regular sādhanā**:
  "Dharma means the attainment of bliss or the endeavour to attain bliss through
  regular sadhana in the subtler spheres of one's nature. This blissful state is
  considered by wise people to be Brahma, and by devotees to be one's very soul."
- **"The word dharma is often loosely used for so-called religion."** Religions claimed
  their doctrines to be "messages of God"; their founders "never followed the path of
  logic," and humanity "lost its supreme treasure, its rationality." Religions preach
  that only their followers are "God's chosen people," confine "universal fraternity
  within the confines of their own community," and "directly or indirectly… encourage
  communalism" (a group psychology based on religion).
- **"Religion tries to transform the human mind into a state of staticity, because
  anything static is easily exploited."** Throughout history religions "proved to be
  flagrantly unworthy institutions, incapable of providing even the physical
  necessities of life."
- **The test of the sages:** *"Yuktiyuktamupādeyam vacanam bālakādapi"* — a rational
  word from a child is accepted, an irrational word from the Supreme Creator is
  rejected; *"Kevalam śāstramāśrityam na karttavyo vinirṇayah"* — blind reliance on
  scripture brings the decline of dharma.

# In *Discourses on Neohumanist Education* (1979–1980) — the four aspects, the three dharmas, and balance

The DNE chapters enrich dharma with its human content (the four aspects), its three
kingdoms (plant/animal/human), and its definition as balance.

- **The quadri-dimensional mānava dharma (dne-14, Let History Be Rewritten, 29 Jan
  1980).** "The quadri-dimensional manava dharma (human dharma) is one and the same. It
  includes: vistara (expansion), rasa (flow), sevá (service) and mokśa (spiritual
  emancipation)." It is equally obligatory for **sannyasins and householders** — the
  only difference is that "householders have to care for two families" (their own and
  the entire universe) "whereas the renunciates have to care for only one."
- **The three dharmas (dne-22, Plants, Animals and Human Beings, 15 Oct 1979).**
  "There are three dharmas – plant dharma, animal dharma and human dharma." **Human
  dharma = Bhāgavata Dharma** = *Ādi Dharma* / *Sanātan Dharma* / Eternal Dharma,
  "for the entire humanity": "Bhagavat Dharma contains no divisions like the division
  between Hindus and Muslims… It makes no distinctions based on isms." Hence the
  slogans *"Mānava mānava ek hyāy"* (All humans are one) and *"Mānav kā dharma ek
  hyāy"* (The dharma of humans is one). Its four aspects: **vistaara** (psychic
  expansion — "the fundamental aim"), **rasa** (flow — "the rása liila or the cosmic
  dance of Parama Puruśa"), **sevá** (service — "I shall give but not take"; human
  beings alone have "the speciality of service," one-sided donation), and **tadsthiti**
  (attainment of the supreme stance).
- **Paradharma is the dharma of plants and animals (dne-22).** The Gītā's counsel:
  "you have to follow human dharma. Be firmly established in it. Even if you meet death
  while following this dharma… But never follow paradharma… This paradharma is the
  dharma of plants and animals." A human following animal dharma (e.g. consuming beef)
  is an aberration; by nature "human beings are not carnivorous. So, if they eat meat
  they will catch numerous diseases."
- **Dharma as balance (dne-12, Genius and Technician, 19 Jun 1979).** "I have already
  said that dharma means the balanced state of all aspects of human life."

# In *Neohumanism in a Nutshell* (1979) — scripture, religion and spirituality

*Human Society Is One and Indivisible – 3* (nh2-6, 17 June 1979) sharpens the
dharma-vs-religion critique:

- **The scripture definition.** *"Śāsanāt tārayet yastu sah śāstra parikīrtitah"* — "That
  which disciplines human beings, that which inspires human beings to follow the path of
  spirituality, the path of supreme benevolence, that is scripture." "Anything else is not
  scripture at all."
- **Religion and logic.** *"Majhabme akkalkā dakhl nehi hae"* [In religion there is no room
  for logical argument.] "Perhaps religion has done the most damage to humanity."
- **Spirituality and religion are separate.** "Spirituality and religion are not synonymous;
  rather they are totally separate entities. Spirituality is an endless endeavour to link the
  microcosm with the Macrocosm."

## Relationships

- goal of dharma = the Great, i.e. [Brahma](/cosmology/brahma.md) / [Ānanda](/cosmology/ananda.md)
- arises from the thirst for [happiness](/biopsychology/sukha.md) made infinite
- practised as dharma sādhanā (cf. [Samādhi](/practice/samadhi.md) as its fruit)
- three tiers: vastu / jaeva / Bhāgavata dharma — only Bhāgavata (= mānava) dharma is consciously practised
- *niiti* (ethics) is its preliminary gate; the ten characteristics (dhṛti…akrodha) draw on [karma](/philosophy/karma.md), [indriya](/biopsychology/indriya.md)-control and [vidyā](/cosmology/vidya-avidya.md)
- svadharma expounded by [Krṣṇa](/people/krsna.md) in the Gītā; dharma's decline summons [Tāraka Brahma](/cosmology/tarakabrahma.md)
- *Elementary Philosophy*: living by dharma = Vidyāmāyā (good deeds) via [viveka](/philosophy/viveka.md) + vaerāgya, against Avidyāmāyā's six enemies & eight fetters; fulfilled through [bhakti](/practice/bhakti.md)
- *Human Society* (1959): dharma ≠ "religion"; morality (*niiti*'s spirit) is society's starting point and the "gate" — cf. [Moralism](/philosophy/moralism.md)
- *DNE 14*: the quadri-dimensional mānava dharma (vistara, rasa, seva, moksa) is "one and the same" for sannyasins and householders
- *DNE 22*: Bhāgavata Dharma = Ādi/Sanātan/Eternal Dharma "for the entire humanity"; seva = "I shall give but not take" — cf. [Seva](/practice/seva.md); paradharma = plant/animal dharma (Gītā)
- *DNE 12*: "dharma means the balanced state of all aspects of human life" — cf. [Pramā](/philosophy/prama.md)
- *nh2-6 (1979)*: "Śāsanāt tārayet yastu sah śāstra parikīrtitah" — scripture is that which disciplines and leads to liberation; "Majhabme akkalkā dakhl nehi hae"; "Perhaps religion has done the most damage to humanity"; spirituality and religion are "totally separate entities" — cf. the [Moralism](/philosophy/moralism.md) (hs-1) critique

# Notes — fidelity

- Sarkar's *dharma* is **not** "religion" or sect, nor "duty/social law" in the
  ordinary sense — it is the innate movement toward the Infinite. Preserve this
  definition; do not normalise toward a generic "religion" gloss.
- **Human Society 1/1** sharpens the anti-religion critique (religions "flagrantly
  unworthy institutions," "anything static is easily exploited") and the sages'
  rational test — direct teaching, keep it sharp.
- **Could not verify in the cited passage:** *Moralism* does not use the niiti/dharma
  ten-characteristics material (SS 21); it speaks of *niiti* as "the principle of
  leading" — the two framings are compatible, kept distinct.
- **nh2-6 (1979) gives the scripture/religion/spirituality three-fold** — the scripture
  śloka and the "Majhabme akkalkā dakhl nehi hae" slogan are quoted verbatim with their
  translations; "Perhaps religion has done the most damage to humanity" and the
  "totally separate entities" statement are direct teaching.

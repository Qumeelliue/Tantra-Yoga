# Definition

**Mudrā** is "the externalization of internal feeling" and means "to express the
desires of the mind": "To communicate ideas through gesture and posture is called
'mudrā'." The fine arts came into existence through mudrā — "Lord Shiva is the
creator of the fine arts." The shloka extends the term to a moral act, the shunning
of wicked company:

> "Sátsaungena bhavenmuktih asatsaungesu bandhanam
> Asatsauṋgamudrańaḿ yat sá mudrá parikiirtitá"
> — "Keeping good company leads to salvation, whereas the company of bad people
> leads to greater bondage. Shunning the company of wicked people is called
> 'mudrā'."

# Explanation

**Three ways of study.** There are "three ways of studying": first, studying using
the eyes; second, studying by hearing ("in the olden times, many people were not
literate but they knew many things by hearing them"); third, "studying by mudrā or
movement" — the "tactual method," by which a blind person knows something by
touching it and the deaf and dumb communicate. If "one indriya (sense organ) ceases
to work, then the others become more sensitive": if each of five organs has a power
of 20 degrees and one ceases, the remaining organs get 25 degrees each.

**The fine arts and Lord Shiva.** "The fine arts came into existence through the
medium of mudrā. Lord Shiva is the creator of the fine arts."

**Mudrā subtler than rhythm.** "In the East, we find that dance is dominated by
mudrā, and in the West, we find that it is dominated by rhythm. Indian folk dance
and classical dance are also mudrā-dominant. Mudrā is more subtle than rhythm."
"In Tantra, mudrā is dominant. Rhythm is guided by mudrā." Mudrās appear in the
Vedas too (auṋkush mudrā in śrāddha; namah, abhaya, varadā mudrās in pitr yajiṋa) —
in the portion made in India. "Instrumental music is also based on mudrā."

**The two dance traditions.** The two types of dance in India — "Aryavarta Nrtya or
North Indian style" and "Dákśińátya Nrtya or Carnatic or the South Indian style" —
"were given by Lord Shiva, but credit for bringing them to the public goes to
Maharshi Bharata." In "the táńd́ava nrtya of Lord Shiva, there is more rhythm, and in
Parvatii's lalita dance there is the dominance of mudrā, sweet and refined." Their
union yields the word *tā́l*: "'tá' is derived from táńd́ava and 'la' from lalita, so
it has become tá'l. Tá'l is the adjustment of both."

## Relationships

- mudrā is "dominant" in [Tantra](/practice/tantra.md) — "Rhythm is guided by mudrā"
- the mudrā-dominant *lalita* of Pārvatī stands against the rhythm-dominant [Tāṇḍava](/practice/tandava.md); their union is *tāla*
- mudrā, singing and instrumental playing together constitute [Sāṁgīta](/practice/samgiita.md), the fine arts created by Lord Shiva
- the "tactual method" is one of the three ways of [education](/philosophy/education.md), hinging on the [indriya](/biopsychology/indriya.md)
- as the shunning of bad company, mudrā leads to [moksa](/practice/moksa.md) ("Sátsaungena bhavenmuktih")

# Notes — fidelity

- **Two senses preserved:** the fine-arts sense ("externalization of internal
  feeling," "communicate ideas through gesture and posture") and the shloka's moral
  sense (shunning wicked company = *mudrā*) are both kept — not collapsed into one.
- **Creator-claim kept as teaching:** "Lord Shiva is the creator of the fine arts"
  is reproduced as Sarkar's direct statement, not as verifiable art history;
  "credit for bringing them to the public goes to Maharshi Bharata."
- **Shloka transliteration:** kept in the source edition's spelling (Satsaungena,
  sauṋga, mudrańam, parikiirtitá) without normalisation.
- **Could not verify:** the claim that "mudrā is more subtle than rhythm" is given
  without a definition of the standard of subtlety; reproduced as Sarkar's framing.

# Definition

**Sāṁgīta** (music) is the togetherness of the three arts: "Singing, the playing of
instruments and dancing together are called 'saḿgiita'." Where dance is absent it is
**giita** — "Yá Bhagavatá giitá sá Giitá," "Whatever Bhagaván has said is Giitá."
There are "six primary rágas and thirty six primary rágińiis." The fine arts were
created by Lord Shiva, who is "the creator of the fine arts."

# Explanation

**Giita vs sāṁgīta.** "Where there is only singing and the playing of instruments but
no dance, it is called 'giita'." In the time of the Mahābhārata, "what Krśńa said was
called 'Giitá' because He only told it, He did not dance. If Krśńa had explained the
nature of Dharma while dancing, then it would not have been called 'Giitá', it would
have been called 'Saḿgiita'."

**The rāga system.** "There are six primary rágas and thirty six primary rágińiis."

**East mudrā-dominant, West rhythm-dominant.** "In the East, we find that dance is
dominated by mudrā, and in the West, we find that it is dominated by rhythm. Indian
folk dance and classical dance are also mudrā-dominant. Mudrā is more subtle than
rhythm." The two styles of Indian dance — Aryavarta Nrtya (North) and Dákśińátya
Nrtya (South) — were both "given by Lord Shiva," popularized by Maharshi Bharata.
The rhythm-dominant táńd́ava of Shiva and the mudrā-dominant *lalita* of Parvatii give
the word *tā́l* ("tá" from táńd́ava, "la" from lalita).

# In *Varna Vijinana* (1983) — the seven notes, the dhrupadii, and the scale

The *Varna Vijinana* series grounds music in the same psycho-acoustic substrate and
theorizes the dhrupadii/kheyál contrast:

- **The seven-note scale underlies all languages and music.** (vv-9) The
  psycho-acoustic notes are "chiefly divided into seven partial sounds which we
  call the seven note scale"; "the creation of all languages and dialects, is
  produced from permutations and combinations of this seven note scale." "Shiva
  took the help of these fundamental sounds to create the six ragas and
  thirty-six raginis of Indian classical music."
- **Dhrupadii — a language or music bound fast by rules.** (vv-13) "That which is
  mastered by continued arduous practice is called dhrupada. The Sanskrit language
  has been bound by the composition of grammar. For this reason, the Sanskrit
  language is called dhrupadii language." (Other dhrupadii languages: old Arabic,
  Hebrew, Latin, old Greek and Pali; Vedic is not, since it has no grammar.) The
  same term names a musical style "where the music is fixed in a certain scale."
  The *kheyál* — "the music's kheyál" — was the Mughal-court freedom "to go here
  and there" within the correct scale, which "saved the music from monotony."
- **From dhrupadii to folk — and back.** (vv-13) Unbound saḿgiita is folk music;
  "when it becomes bound by certain rules, it will also rise to the level of
  dhrupadii, as has happened with Manipurii dance." This rising has two sides:
  it gains prestige but "this respect and prestige prevent it from moving ahead on
  its path. The flowing river full of vitality gets transformed into a swamp
  choked with water hyacinths – the sun of its fortune sinks forever into the mud
  and mire."
- **Sanskrit as fixed-fret sitar.** (vv-13) Kabir is cited: "Saḿskrta kúpodaka,
  bhákhá vahatá niira" (Sanskrit is like well-water, the mother tongue like
  flowing river-water). "Sanskrit is like a sitar with its frets fixed while the
  people's language is like a violin without fixed frets – if you can stop the
  strings, though, it sounds very melodious."
- **Language and the musical scale obey the same law.** (vv-7) Linguistic
  transformation "is similar to the rules of transformation which govern the scale
  in classical music. There is little scope for deviation." As in the Kheyál style,
  "although there is an opportunity for vocal play… in the end one has to return
  to the set scale usage."

## Relationships

- sāṁgīta is singing + instruments + dance; its gesture-and-posture element is [Mudrā](/practice/mudra.md) ("mudrā is more subtle than rhythm")
- the dance member includes the rhythm-dominant [Tāṇḍava](/practice/tandava.md) of Lord Shiva
- devotional music is realised in [Kiirtana](/practice/kiirtana.md), the mantra-song of the path of devotion
- its vocal root is the seed-sound [Omkāra](/cosmology/omkara.md)
- as the "fine arts created by Lord Shiva," it carries the sentiment of [bhakti](/practice/bhakti.md)
- one of the three ways of [education](/philosophy/education.md) is "studying by hearing," alongside eyes and mudrā
- *VV 9*: the seven-note scale is the substrate of all languages and music; "Shiva took the help of these fundamental sounds to create the six ragas and thirty-six raginis of Indian classical music"
- *VV 13*: the **dhrupadii** doctrine — Sanskrit is "dhrupadii language" (bound by grammar); dhrupadii → kheyál (the "freedom to go here and there" within the scale); folk → dhrupadii loses vitality ("a swamp choked with water hyacinths"); "Sanskrit is like a sitar with its frets fixed"
- *VV 7*: language transformation follows "the rules of transformation which govern the scale in classical music" — in the end "one has to return to the set scale usage" (the Kheyál analogy, cf. [Acoustic Roots](/cosmology/acoustic-roots.md))

# Notes — fidelity

- **Distinction kept exact:** giita = singing + instruments (no dance); sāṁgīta = all three. The Krśńa / Giitá example preserves the source's reasoning that dancing would have made it "Saḿgiita."
- **Numbers as given:** "six primary rágas and thirty six primary rágińiis" is stated without enumeration — reproduced as-is, no further list invented.
- **Creator-claim:** "the fine arts created by Lord Shiva" is a direct teaching; attribution of popularization to "Maharshi Bharata" is kept separate.
- **Could not verify:** the identity of the six primary rāgas / thirty-six primary rāgiṇīs, and the historical details of Maharshi Bharata, are not expanded in the cited passage.
- **VV corroboration (1983):** VV 9 confirms the six-rāgas/thirty-six-rāgiṇīs count and grounds them in the seven-note psycho-acoustic scale ("Shiva took the help of these fundamental sounds"); VV 13 supplies the dhrupadii/kheyál contrast verbatim (with the well/river Kabir verse and the sitar/violin analogy); VV 7 draws the parallel between linguistic transformation and the classical scale. The *Samgiita* section of *Talks on Education – Excerpt D* (1970) and the VV passages are separate discourses — kept as distinct sources.

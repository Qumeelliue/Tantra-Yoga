# Definition

**Yama and Niyama** is Sarkar's name for the ten-fold cardinal moral code — glossed in
the corpus simply as **"the moral code"** and **"ten principles of moral conduct."** It
constitutes the **first two limbs of the eight-limbed (aṣṭāṅga) yoga** and is the
indispensable foundation on which sādhanā stands: *"the initial struggle that a sādhaka
has to wage, standing firmly on the ground of yama and niyama"* (SS 5). Sarkar treats it
as part of Brahma Sādhanā itself: *"every item of Yama-Niyama is part of Brahma Sādhanā
through worldly deeds"* (SS 1).

# Explanation

**Morality precedes meditation.** In *The Only Way to Salvation* (SS 5) Sarkar grounds
the whole spiritual effort on this code: the human mind is first **"washed with the holy
water of yama and niyama (the first two limbs of the eight-limbed aṣṭāṅga yoga),"** and
only then can the "heat of sādhanā" burn away its impurities — as impure gold is smelted
only after washing. Standing firmly on yama-niyama is identified with the perfecting of
*pratyāhāra* sādhanā (withdrawal of mind from crude objects).

**Yama-Niyama as Brahma Sādhanā in the world.** In *The Form of Sādhana* (SS 1) Sarkar
denies that morality breeds world-apathy: *"No attachment to worldly objects will be
developed if we observe Yama and Niyama, since every item of Yama-Niyama is part of Brahma
Sādhanā through worldly deeds."* It is therefore practised within family and social life,
not in forest seclusion — and when perfected "your mind becomes absorbed in Brahma."

**The prerequisite for responsibility and leadership.** *The Conduct of an Ācārya* (AV 31)
and *The Macrocosmic Stance and Human Life* (SS 7) make establishment in yama-niyama the
precondition for social trust: *"Only those who are established in Yama and Niyama … and
practise sādhanā can be given any responsibility";* those who lead the social struggle
"must be established in yama and niyama … Such people I call **sadvipras**."

**The ten members.** Yama-niyama is the five **yama** (ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya,
aparigraha) and five **niyama** (shaoca, santoṣa, tapaḥ, svādhyāya, iishvara-praṇidhāna),
defined at length in Sarkar's *[A Guide to Human Conduct](/works/guide-to-human-conduct/001-a-guide-to-human-conduct.md)*
— **now ingested in this bundle**, so each member is grounded individually in the section
below. The *prose discourses* (SS/AV) still treat the code as a *unit*; the one member they
gloss directly is
**tapaḥ**: *"undergoing trouble or accepting physical discomforts in order to serve others
or to reach one's spiritual goal"* (AV 23 glossary) — Sarkar's service-oriented sense, not
generic "austerity."

## Relationships

- the first two of the eight limbs of [aṣṭāṅga yoga](/practice/yoga.md) — the moral base on which pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, [dhyāna](/practice/dhyana.md) and samādhi are built
- indispensable prerequisite and integral part of [sādhanā](/practice/sadhana.md) — "the initial struggle," the "holy water" that washes the mind before the heat of practice
- **distinct from** the ten characteristics of [dharma](/philosophy/dharma.md) (dhṛti, kṣamā, dama, asteya, shaoca, indriya-nigraha, dhī, vidyā, satya, akrodha) — a SEPARATE decalogue (from the shloka "…dashakaṃ dharmalakṣaṇam", AV 8) that overlaps yama-niyama only on *asteya, shaoca, satya*
- establishment in yama-niyama qualifies one for social [service](/practice/seva.md) and leadership — the defining trait of the [sadvipra](/prout/sadvipra.md)
- part of the practical [Tantra](/practice/tantra.md) / yogic cult; complements the discriminating faculty of [viveka](/philosophy/viveka.md)
- enforced behaviourally by the [Fifteen Shiilas](/practice/fifteen-shiilas.md) (Shiila 10 = "strict adherence to the principles of Yama and Niyama") and the [Conduct Rules](/practice/conduct-rules.md)

# In *A Guide to Human Conduct* — the ten members defined

Sarkar's *A Guide to Human Conduct* (Ānanda Pūrṇimā 1957) defines all ten members and
corrects their common misreadings. **Morality is the *foundation* of sādhanā, not its
goal** — "a dynamic force," a "pauseless war against all opposite ideas," "the starting
point on the path of Sādhanā," never its culmination. The aim is to eliminate the very
*tendency* (e.g. to steal or lie), reaching oneness with Brahma.

**The Yama shloka** — *Ahiṃsāsatyāsteyaṃ Brahmacaryāparigrahāh yamah*:

- **Ahiṃsā** — not inflicting pain or hurt on anybody by thought, word or action, judged
  by the *intention* to harm. NOT the extreme "kill nothing" reading and NOT mere
  non-violence ("the mark of … ahiṃsā on a bullet does not make the bullet non-violent");
  force against an aggressor (*ātatāyii*) and rectifying punishment are not hiṃsā —
  "desisting from such use of force is cowardice."
- **Satya** — proper action of mind and right use of words **with the spirit of welfare**
  (*parahitārtham*); benevolent truth, NOT bare fact (*rta*). Misdirecting a murderer from
  his victim still "maintains the dignity of Satya"; its finality is Parama Brahma.
- **Asteya** — not taking what belongs to others; fourfold (physical/psychic theft,
  physically/mentally depriving others of their due). Even "ticketless travel amounts to theft."
- **Brahmacarya** — "to remain attached to Brahma" (*Brahmaṇi vicaraṇam*): treating every
  object contacted as an expression of Brahma. NOT preservation of semen ("neither … Brahma
  nor … carya has any relevance to … 'semen'") — that reading was "a bluff" and a tool of
  monastic exploitation; marriage is "a natural function like bath, food, sleep."
- **Aparigraha** — non-indulgence in comforts superfluous to the preservation of life; the
  *relative* minimum varies with time, person and place — "an endless fight to reduce one's
  own objects of comforts out of sympathy for the common people," not a regression to primitivism.

**The five Niyama** (introduced discursively — Sarkar gives **no composite Niyama shloka**):

- **Shaoca** — purity, of two kinds: external (body, clothes, surroundings) and internal
  (removing the mind's "dirts," chiefly selfishness and jealousy), achieved by selfless service.
- **Santoṣa** — from *toṣa*, "mental ease": contentment with the earnings of normal labour.
  It does NOT mean tolerating exploitation — one must still fight for legitimate rights.
- **Tapaḥ** — penance to reach the goal: **shouldering others' sorrows to make them happy**,
  service above selfishness; free of commercial mentality and **requiring discrimination**
  ("Tapah devoid of knowledge is bound to be misused" — serve the weak, not the powerful).
  This corroborates the AV 23 service-gloss above.
- **Svādhyāya** — clear *understanding* of a spiritual subject, NOT rote reading without
  grasping the meaning (illustrated by the *māṃsa sādhanā* reinterpretation: "māṃsa" =
  control of the tongue/speech, not animal sacrifice).
- **Iishvara Praṇidhāna** — to establish oneself in the Cosmic idea, accepting Iishvara as
  the only ideal of life; an entirely mental effort of ideation ("Your Iishvara is not deaf —
  don't shout"; **mental japa is its best form**). Practised individually or collectively,
  the collective giving "indomitable mental force" — hence weekly dharmacakra.

**Structure of the code.** Of the ten, the other eight "are subordinated parts of the two
items, **Brahmacarya and Iishvara Praṇidhāna**"; Brahmacarya is "held in higher esteem than
the other four items of Yama," and Iishvara praṇidhāna is the most important Niyama (carrying
"equal weight in mundane, supramundane and spiritual strata"). The universal practice-method
for all ten is **auto-suggestion**, and their collective practice is "the sound foundation of
an ideal society." (All claims in this section: ghc-1.)

# In *Idea and Ideology* (1959) — the sadvipra's ten commands

The 1959 chapter *The Place of Sadvipras in the Samája Cakra* (II-10) defines all
ten members in its account of the social role of the [sadvipra](/prout/sadvipra.md)
— "spiritual revolutionaries who work to achieve such progressive changes for human
elevation … by adhering to the principles of Yama and Niyama":

- **Yama:** *ahiṃsā* — "not causing suffering to any harmless creature through
  thought, word or deed"; *satya* — "action of mind or use of words with the object
  of helping others in the real sense. It has no relative application"; *asteya* —
  non-stealing "not confined to physical action but [extended] to the action of the
  mind as well… the correct sense of asteya is 'to give up the desire of acquiring
  what is not rightly one's own'"; *aparigraha* — "the non-acceptance of such
  amenities and comforts of life as are superfluous for the preservation of the
  physical existence"; *brahmacarya* — "to experience His presence and authority in
  each and every physical and psychic objectivity," which occurs "when the unit mind
  resonates with Cosmic will."
- **Niyama:** *shaoca* — purity of both physical and mental bodies, mental purity by
  benevolent deeds, charity and dutiful acts; *santoṣa* — "contentment… accepting
  ungrudgingly and without a complaint the out-turn of the services rendered by
  one's own physical or mental labour"; *tapaḥ* — "efforts to reach the goal despite
  such efforts being associated with physical discomforts"; *svādhyāya* — "study of
  the scriptures or other books of learning and assimilating their spirit";
  *Iishvara praṇidhāna* — "an auto-suggestion of the idea that each and every unit
  is an instrument in the hands of the Almighty and is a mere spark of that supreme
  fire," implying "implicit faith in Him irrespective of whether one lives in
  momentary happiness or sorrow, prosperity or adversity."
- **Only those who by their nature adhere to these ten commands in their normal and
  spiritual conduct are sadvipras** — the moral-spiritual qualification of the
  nucleus-leadership (cf. [Sadvipra](/prout/sadvipra.md)).

# In the *Discourses on Neohumanist Education* (1959) — the sadvipra's ten commands (DNE 26)

*The Place of Sadvipras in the Samája Cakra* (DNE 26, 4 June 1959) is the **same
discourse** as Idea and Ideology II-10 (see the section above); its DNE print
repeats the ten-command definitions verbatim, corroborating the II-10 wording:

- **Yama:** *ahiṃsā* — "not causing suffering to any harmless creature through
  thought, word or deed"; *satya* — "action of mind or use of words with the
  object of helping others in the real sense. It has no relative application";
  *asteya* — "the correct sense of asteya is 'to give up the desire of acquiring
  what is not rightly one's own'"; *aparigraha* — "the non-acceptance of such
  amenities and comforts of life as are superfluous for the preservation of the
  physical existence"; *Brahmacarya* — "to experience His [the Supreme Entity's]
  presence and authority in each and every physical and psychic objectivity. This
  occurs when the unit mind resonates with Cosmic will."
- **Niyama:** *shaoca*, *santoṣa*, *tapaḥ*, *svādhyāya*, and *Iishvara
  praṇidhāna* — "an auto-suggestion of the idea that each and every unit is an
  instrument in the hands of the Almighty and is a mere spark of that supreme
  fire," implying "implicit faith in Him irrespective of whether one lives in
  momentary happiness or sorrow, prosperity or adversity."
- **The qualification:** "Only those who by their nature adhere to the above ten
  commands in their normal and spiritual conduct are sadvipras" (cf.
  [Sadvipra](/prout/sadvipra.md)).

# In *Neohumanism in a Nutshell* (1982) — ahiṃsā and rational food

*Ahiṃsā in Neohumanism* (nh2-8, 21 February 1982) states the ahiṃsā-food tension
briefly (the same locust anecdote is treated under [Bhakti](/practice/bhakti.md) and
[Neohumanism](/philosophy/neohumanism.md)):

- **Rational behaviour.** "Obviously human beings will have to behave rationally; they must
  maintain their existence while adjusting with the external environment."
- **Living food.** "It is true that living creatures are the food for other living beings
  (jiivah jiivasya bhojanam); and indeed, the vegetables that we eat every day also have
  living cells in them." — with a pointer to his books *A Guide to Human Conduct* (1961) and
  *Caryācarya Part 3* (1965) for the food doctrine.

# Notes — fidelity

- **Grounded COLLECTIVELY only.** This bundle treats yama-niyama as a unit ("moral code",
  "ten principles of moral conduct", "first two limbs of aṣṭāṅga yoga", the "holy water"
  prerequisite of sādhanā, the sadvipra qualification) — all verbatim-grounded. It does
  **not** enumerate the ten members as a list or define them individually.
- The ten members are now **grounded individually** in
  *[A Guide to Human Conduct](/works/guide-to-human-conduct/001-a-guide-to-human-conduct.md)*
  (see the section above) — definitions, defining shlokas and Sarkar's misreading-corrections.
  That work gives a Yama shloka but **no composite Niyama shloka**, and makes no mention of
  Patañjali or "aṣṭāṅga / eight limbs" — the aṣṭāṅga framing stays grounded in the SS/AV
  works cited, not in GHC.
- **tapaḥ** is the one member with a grounded gloss (AV 23) — reproduced exactly; do not
  replace its service sense with conventional "austerity."
- **Distinct decalogues:** keep yama-niyama separate from dharma's "ten characteristics"
  (the corpus enumerates/defines the *dharma* list, not the yama-niyama list).
- **Idea and Ideology (1959)** provides a *second, independent* per-member grounding (the
  sadvipra's ten commands, II-10), consistent with GHC but with its own wordings — keep both
  without harmonising the phrasing.
- **nh2-8 (1982)** grounds ahiṃsā's food-tension briefly: "human beings will have to behave
  rationally; they must maintain their existence while adjusting with the external
  environment"; "jiivah jiivasya bhojanam" (living creatures the food of living beings;
  vegetables too "have living cells in them") — cf. [Bhakti](/practice/bhakti.md),
  [Neohumanism](/philosophy/neohumanism.md).

# Definition

**"Anubhūtaviṣayāsaṃpramoṣaḥ smṛtiḥ" — "The re-creation of things already
perceived by the mind is called memory."** (DNE 30, DNE 42.) "The objects or
incidents that one recollects are called anubhūtaviṣaya or things already
perceived. When the same objects or incidents are recreated in the mind, they
are called smṛti or memory."

# Explanation

**The brain and the citta.** "The brain is nothing more than a worldly machine
for mental recollection. Its various parts assist the mind in various ways. But
the permanent abode of memory is the citta." When the brain assists in the
recollection of any event or fact it is called **cerebral memory**. Perception
is registered in the unit mind through the nerve cells, whose vibrations remain
embedded in them; but when the impression fades from the nerve cells, "the
perceived image is stored in the citta or ectoplasmic mind-stuff and not the
brain. Hence the mind will have to labour hard to reformulate the image ... from
the accumulated saṃskāras."

**Memory as re-creation.** The mind "becomes similar and identical" to the
perceived object (anubhava, "to become the same afterwards"); memory is the
revival of that mental formation by will-force. "When you reconvert your mind
into sound, touch, form, taste or smell, it is called smṛti or memory." Thus
memory is distinct from jñāna: one may recreate the dhrupad song in the mind
without understanding what dhrupad actually is. "So memory and jñāna are not
one and the same."

**Extra-cerebral memory.** "The revival of experiences of past lives is what we
call 'extra-cerebral memory', and is principally the task of the causal mind."
After death the disembodied mind "floats in the vast space with its unexpressed
saṃskāras," then finds a suitable physical base. The memory of its past life
remains awake for approximately the first five years of the new life — children
laughing and crying in their sleep, which in colloquial Bengali is called
**deola kāṭā** — then begins to fade. Those who retain it are **jātismara**;
"normally, the extra-cerebral memory of such people remains active up to the
age of twelve," after which two minds clash in one body and "psycho-physical
parallelism is lost leading to eventual death." In sleep the causal mind
dominates: **"Kāraṇamanasi dīrghanidrā maraṇam"** — "Long sleep in the causal
mind is death."

**Forgetfulness — a providential decree.** "Forgetfulness is a providential
decree." It is a blessing: "human beings feel burdened by the weight of one
life. It would be impossible for them to carry the burden of many lives
together." Tagore's *pashcāter āmi* (the "me behind me") "refers to the
extra-cerebral memory": the memory of past actions pulls one back — or, for the
sādhaka, pushes one forward: "Sammukhe ṭheliche more pashcāter āmi." Nothing is
lost: "the history of a person's hopes and frustrations ... remains stored in
their subconscious mind," and "all knowledge, one's entire past history and
flashes of one's previous lives, remain stored in sequential order in the
causal mind." By sādhana one may visualize that panorama.

**Dhruvāsmṛti — constant memory.** "When the memory becomes established,
unfailing and spontaneous, it is called dhruvāsmṛti, or constant memory.
Dhruvāsmṛti, or constant memory, is an essential prerequisite for spiritual
samādhi or bliss." (DNE 42.) Those who "always mentally remember God" enjoy "a
special kind of Ānanda or bliss in their minds. That Ānanda is called
Dharmameghānanda." (DNE 40.)

**Memory techniques.** Rhythm is an ancient memory technology: "Human beings do
not easily forget rhythm. One may forget the contents of knowledge, but not the
rhythm. That is why all Vedic ṛks were composed in 7 metres: Gāyattrī, Uṣṇik,
Triṣṭup, Anuṣṭup, Bṛhatī, Jagatī and Pauṅkti." Reading aloud converts the mind
twice — "once through the eyes, and once through the ears. The result will be
more effective memorization." (DNE 43.) For memorizing, "there should be both a
physical and a psychic approach, and to some extent a spiritual approach," and
one should avoid the use of sub-glands producing inimical hormones. (DNE 35,
see [Bio-Psychology](/biopsychology/bio-psychology.md).)

## Relationships

- the permanent abode of memory is the [Citta](/biopsychology/citta.md)
- memory recreates mental formations from [Saṃskāra](/biopsychology/samskara.md), the reactive momenta of past actions
- the faculty of memory belongs to the [Unit Mind](/biopsychology/unit-mind.md); its three stages (crude, subtle, causal)
- extra-cerebral memory persists across [Death](/biopsychology/death.md), carried in the causal mind
- sādhana suspends the crude mind in the subtle and the subtle in the causal, a power of [Sādhana](/practice/sadhana.md)
- memory is one faculty within the [Faculty of Knowledge](/philosophy/faculty-of-knowledge.md), distinct from jñāna
- dhruvāsmṛti brings Ānanda (Dharmameghānanda), cf. [Ānanda](/cosmology/ananda.md)
- dhruvāsmṛti is a prerequisite for [Samādhi](/practice/samadhi.md)
- memory technique is anchored in glandular/plexus knowledge of [Bio-Psychology](/biopsychology/bio-psychology.md)

# Notes — fidelity

- The Sanskrit aphorism **anubhūtaviṣayāsaṃpramoṣaḥ smṛtiḥ** is quoted exactly
  (DNE 30 and DNE 42 give slightly different transliterations; the IAST form is
  used here, the Bengali pronunciation footnote "Srti" noted in DNE 40 is
  retained in the sources).
- **Deola kāṭā**, **jātismara**, **pashcāter āmi**, **Kāraṇamanasi
  dīrghanidrā maraṇam** and **Dharmameghānanda** are kept verbatim as recorded.
- The dhruvāsmṛti definition and its role as prerequisite for samādhi are
  direct teaching from DNE 42; the Dharmameghānanda bliss is from DNE 40.
- The Tagore verses are quoted in the original with the translation given in
  the source; the "pashcāter āmi" identification with extra-cerebral memory is
  Sarkar's explicit statement.
- DNE 40 uses a simplified spelling ("Anubhuta vishaya sampromoshah smrti");
  the canonical transliteration is retained here.
- Could not verify in the cited passages: a mechanical description of how the
  citta physically "stores" impressions beyond the wave-recreation account, or
  an exact age boundary for the *onset* of jātismara fade beyond "fades after
  five years" / "active up to the age of twelve."
